How to actually read
a P/E ratio

It's the first number most people learn and the one most people misuse. Here's what a P/E ratio is really telling you on the NSE and JSE, and why a low one isn't automatically a bargain.

← Back to all articles
May 2026 · The African Oracle Research Team · Education

If you've spent any time around NSE or JSE investors, you've heard someone justify a purchase with some version of "it's only trading on a P/E of 5, that's cheap." Sometimes they're right. Often they're not. The price-to-earnings ratio is probably the single most quoted number in investing, and also one of the most misunderstood.

So let's actually work through what it means, because once you get past the textbook definition there's a lot more nuance than most people give it credit for — and that nuance matters a great deal on markets like ours, where P/E multiples sit well below what you'd see in London or New York for reasons that have nothing to do with the ratio itself.

The basic definition, quickly

P/E ratio is share price divided by earnings per share. If a stock trades at KES 100 and earned KES 10 per share over the last twelve months, its P/E is 10. Put another way, you're paying 10 times the company's annual profit to own a share of it. Flip it around and the "earnings yield" — the inverse of P/E — is 10%, which is roughly what you'd be earning on your money each year if profits stayed flat and the company paid out everything as dividends.

That's the whole calculation. The interesting part is what a low or high number actually implies, because it is never simply "cheap" or "expensive" in isolation.

A low P/E is a question, not an answer

This is where most people go wrong. A P/E of 5 doesn't mean a stock is cheap. It means the market is only willing to pay five years' worth of current earnings for it. That's a statement about risk and growth expectations, not a verdict on value.

Take a look at NSE banking stocks. KCB Group and Co-operative Bank have both traded around the 4–5x range in recent periods, while Safaricom trades closer to 14–15x. Is Safaricom five times more expensive relative to its earnings? Not really — it's priced for a completely different set of expectations. Safaricom has a dominant, near-monopoly market position in mobile money through M-Pesa, predictable margins, and a market that trusts its earnings will keep compounding. The banks are priced lower partly because bank earnings carry credit risk that's genuinely hard to see from the outside — a loan book can look fine right up until it isn't — and partly because the market is pricing in regional expansion risk, currency exposure in markets like South Sudan or the DRC, and the structurally lower growth ceiling of mature lending books.

A cheap P/E is the market telling you it's worried about something. Your job isn't to ignore that worry — it's to figure out whether the market is right to be worried, or overreacting.

Sometimes the market is wrong, and that mispricing is exactly what value investors are hunting for. Sometimes the market has it exactly right, and that low multiple is a warning sign dressed up as a bargain. The ratio alone can't tell you which situation you're in.

A high P/E isn't automatically expensive either

The same logic runs in reverse. A stock on a P/E of 25 can still be a good buy if its earnings are growing fast enough to "grow into" that multiple within a couple of years. This is really the entire premise behind the PEG ratio, which we cover in a separate article — it adjusts the P/E for growth so you're comparing like with like.

A company growing earnings at 25% a year on a P/E of 20 can be considerably cheaper, in a real economic sense, than a company growing at 3% a year on a P/E of 8. The second one might look "cheaper" on the headline number and be the worse investment by a wide margin.

Why African market P/Es sit lower than developed markets

This is the bit that trips up investors comparing NSE or JSE stocks to US or European peers. The entire Kenyan market trades at a meaningful discount to its own long-run historical average, and both the NSE and JSE typically trade at lower multiples than developed exchanges — not because African companies are worse businesses, but because of a handful of structural factors that get baked into every valuation:

  • Liquidity risk. If you need to sell a large position quickly on a thinly traded NSE counter, you may move the price against yourself. Investors demand a discount for that.
  • Currency risk. The Kenyan shilling and South African rand have both had periods of meaningful volatility against the dollar. A foreign investor pricing in currency risk will pay less for the same earnings stream than they would for an equivalent company reporting in dollars or euros.
  • Political and regulatory risk. Policy can shift quickly — tax changes, new levies, regulatory interventions in banking or telecoms — and markets price that uncertainty in via a lower multiple.
  • Cost of capital. Interest rates in Kenya and South Africa have historically run well above those in the US or Eurozone. Since a stock's fair multiple is mathematically tied to the discount rate used to value future cash flows, higher local interest rates mechanically compress fair P/E ratios.

None of this means NSE and JSE stocks are bad investments — quite the opposite, since a lower average multiple can mean more room for re-rating if any of those risk factors ease. It just means you shouldn't benchmark a Kenyan bank's P/E against a European bank's P/E and conclude the Kenyan bank is "cheaper." They're not really comparable without adjusting for the underlying risk.

What P/E can't tell you at all

A few things the ratio is structurally blind to:

  • Debt levels. Two companies can have identical P/E ratios while one carries almost no debt and the other is leveraged to the hilt. The P/E says nothing about the balance sheet. That's what metrics like debt-to-equity and interest cover are for — we go into this in our piece on financial strength.
  • Earnings quality. Earnings can be flattered by one-off gains, asset revaluations, or accounting choices that won't repeat. A P/E built on an inflated earnings number will understate how expensive a stock really is.
  • Negative earnings. If a company is loss-making, the P/E ratio simply doesn't work — you'll either see a meaningless negative number or "n/a." You need other tools entirely for early-stage or turnaround businesses.
  • Capital intensity. A capital-light business generating KES 1 billion in profit is worth more than a capital-heavy one generating the same profit, because the capital-light business needs to reinvest less to keep growing. P/E treats both identically.

How we'd actually use it

In practice, P/E is a starting filter, not a conclusion. A sensible process looks something like: compare a stock's P/E to its own five- or ten-year average first, since that tells you whether it's cheap or expensive relative to itself. Then compare it to direct sector peers facing similar risks — Equity Group against KCB and Co-op, not against a Nigerian bank or a European one. Then ask what the market is pricing in and whether you agree with that assessment. Only after all of that does the number start to mean something useful.

Used that way, alongside PEG, dividend yield, debt levels and a sense of the company's competitive position, P/E remains one of the most useful shortcuts an investor has. Used in isolation — "cheap P/E, must be a bargain" — it will mislead you as often as it helps you.
