How to actually read a P/E ratio
on African stocks

The most cited number in investing. Also one of the most misused. Here is what it genuinely tells you — and the situations where it will lead you badly astray.

← Back to all articles
May 2026 · The African Oracle Research Team · NSE & JSE

Pick any financial website covering NSE or JSE stocks and you will find the P/E ratio front and centre. Investors cite it constantly. Financial journalists lead with it. It is the first thing people check before buying a share — which is partly why it gets misused so often.

The formula is simple enough. Take the share price, divide by the earnings per share, and you get the P/E ratio. If Safaricom trades at KES 27.50 and its EPS is KES 1.74, the P/E is roughly 15.8. That number tells you that you are paying 15.8 years worth of current earnings to own one share.

What it does not tell you — and this is where most people go wrong — is whether that is cheap or expensive. Not without context.

Why P/E comparisons require a reference point

Imagine two companies. Both have a P/E of 8. On the surface, they look equally cheap. But one is a Kenyan commercial bank generating a 30% return on equity with a clean loan book and a five-year earnings growth rate of 22%. The other is a struggling manufacturer with declining revenues, a single customer that accounts for 60% of sales, and a balance sheet carrying debt it can barely service. Same P/E ratio. Completely different situations.

The number means nothing in isolation. It only starts to mean something when you compare it to:

  • The company's own historical P/E (is it cheap relative to where it usually trades?)
  • Its sector peers (is it cheaper or more expensive than comparable businesses?)
  • The broader market average (is the whole market expensive, making everything look deceptively cheap by relative comparison?)
  • The company's growth rate (this is where the PEG ratio becomes essential, but more on that in a separate article)
A P/E of 5 on the NSE is not automatically cheap. It might be cheap. Or it might be the market's way of telling you something you have not figured out yet.

African market P/E realities

There is a persistent narrative that African stocks are undervalued because they trade at lower P/E multiples than US or European equivalents. This is partly true and partly misleading.

The NSE's overall market P/E has historically ranged between 8 and 18 depending on economic conditions. The JSE's overall market P/E has typically ranged between 10 and 22. Both are structurally lower than US markets, where the S&P 500 regularly trades above 25. But the reason for this discount is not simply that African companies are ignored by global investors and will therefore "catch up".

The discount reflects real factors. Higher country risk. Currency volatility. Smaller market liquidity. Political uncertainty in various African countries. Weaker regulatory protection for minority shareholders in some markets. These are not irrational fears — they are genuine risks that investors price into the multiples they are willing to pay.

What this means practically: you should not assume an NSE bank trading at a P/E of 6 is undervalued just because European banks trade at 10 or 12. The comparison is legitimate only if the underlying business quality, growth trajectory, and risk profile are genuinely similar. Sometimes they are. But often they are not.

When P/E is genuinely useful on the NSE and JSE

All of that said, the P/E ratio is a genuinely useful tool when you use it correctly. Here are the situations where it is most reliable as a signal.

Comparing companies in the same sector

Take the NSE banking sector. As of mid-2026, KCB trades at a P/E of around 4 to 5. Equity Group trades at roughly 5 to 6. Standard Chartered Kenya trades somewhere above 12. All three are commercial banks in Kenya. All three operate under the same regulatory environment. Why the difference?

Standard Chartered Kenya's premium reflects its higher return on equity, its international parentage, and its corporate banking focus which generates more stable, fee-heavy revenue. KCB's discount reflects its larger exposure to the DRC and other higher-risk regional markets, plus lingering market scepticism about its acquisition strategy. Neither of these P/E ratios is simply "cheap" or "expensive" in the abstract — both are rational market assessments of the different risk and return profiles of each business.

The comparison is valuable precisely because the businesses are in the same sector. You are controlling for macro factors and regulatory environment, which makes the P/E differences more attributable to company-specific factors that you can actually analyse.

Tracking a single company over time

If you have been following Safaricom for five years, you know that it has historically traded between P/E multiples of 12 and 22. When it dips toward the lower end of that range — usually during a period of market-wide fear or a company-specific earnings disappointment — that context tells you something useful that a single snapshot number cannot.

This is why long-term investors who have built up institutional knowledge about specific companies can use P/E effectively in a way that first-time investors cannot. The number is most useful when you understand the history behind it.

Where the P/E ratio fails you completely

There are some situations where you should not use P/E at all, or at least not without significant modification.

Companies making losses. If a company is reporting negative earnings, the P/E ratio is either undefined or negative — neither of which gives you usable information. Kenya Airways (KQ) is the obvious NSE example. Analysing KQ using P/E is pointless. You need to look at the path to profitability, the balance sheet, and the debt restructuring situation instead.

Highly cyclical businesses. JSE mining companies are the best example here. A gold miner's earnings in a year when gold is at $3,000 per ounce are very different from its earnings when gold is at $1,800. The P/E at the bottom of a gold cycle looks horrifying but might actually be a great time to buy. The P/E at the top of the cycle looks attractive but the earnings are not sustainable. Analysts deal with this using "through-the-cycle" earnings estimates rather than trailing P/E.

Companies with very different capital structures. A company carrying R10 billion of debt looks similar in P/E terms to a debt-free company with the same earnings — but they are fundamentally different investments. The debt-laden company's earnings are far more fragile if interest rates rise or revenues fall. This is why looking at P/E alongside the debt-to-equity ratio and interest coverage is essential, not optional.

The growth dimension: why P/E without growth context is incomplete

A company growing earnings at 25% per year deserves a higher P/E than a company with flat earnings. This seems obvious, but the P/E ratio does not tell you which one you are looking at. That is why Peter Lynch popularised the PEG ratio — P/E divided by the earnings growth rate — as a way of putting the P/E in growth context.

On the NSE, you will find companies with P/E ratios of 4 or 5 that have flat or declining earnings — and companies with P/E ratios of 10 or 12 that are growing earnings at 20% annually. On a PEG basis, the second group might actually be the better value despite looking more expensive on a raw P/E comparison.

We cover the PEG ratio in detail in a separate article. But the short version: whenever you are using P/E to assess value, ask yourself what the earnings growth rate is. If you cannot answer that question, the P/E number is only telling you half the story.

A practical framework for using P/E on African stocks

Here is how we think about it when we are running through the screener data:

Below 8: Flag for investigation. Not automatically cheap — find out why it is this low. Is the sector structurally impaired? Is there a specific company-level problem? Is the market just broadly cheap right now?

8 to 15: Reasonable range for most profitable African companies. Within this band, you are making a judgement about the quality and growth rate of the underlying business.

15 to 25: The market expects something. Earnings growth, a turnaround, a sector re-rating. Make sure you understand what the market is pricing in before you pay this multiple.

Above 25: You are paying for a very specific story about the future. The company needs to deliver material earnings growth for this to look reasonable in hindsight. JSE tech and consumer companies sometimes sit here. Scrutinise carefully.

These are rough guides, not rules. An NSE bank at 20x P/E might make sense if it is genuinely growing at 30% per year with strong returns on equity. A consumer staple at 8x might be a value trap if the category it operates in is structurally shrinking.

The P/E ratio is a starting point. It is the first question, not the last answer. Use it to rank companies for further investigation, not to make final buy or sell decisions on its own.
