The NSE in 2026:
what's actually driving the market

A market at record highs, a central bank cutting rates, and banking stocks doing most of the heavy lifting. Here's what the data is telling us.

← Back to all articles
May 2026 · The African Oracle Research Team · NSE Kenya

The Nairobi Securities Exchange has had a genuinely strong run into 2026, with the NASI — the broad All Share Index — pushing to record territory, comfortably above the 200-point mark, in February. For a market that spent a good stretch of the previous few years in a fairly grinding bear phase, that's worth pausing on. What's actually behind it, and does it mean the easy money has already been made?

The macro backdrop: lower rates doing a lot of the work

The single biggest driver behind the rally has been monetary policy. The Central Bank of Kenya has been on an easing path, bringing the Central Bank Rate down toward the 9% region — a meaningful drop from the tighter policy stance of recent years. Lower interest rates matter enormously for equities through two separate channels: they reduce the discount rate used to value future company earnings, which mechanically supports higher valuations, and they make bonds and money market instruments relatively less attractive, pushing some investor capital back toward equities in search of better returns.

A more stable shilling has reinforced this. Currency stability reduces one of the biggest risk premiums foreign and local investors alike attach to Kenyan assets, and a calmer currency backdrop has genuinely helped sentiment across the market, not just in equities.

Valuation: still cheap relative to its own history

Despite the rally, the market as a whole has continued to trade at a meaningful discount to its own long-run historical average P/E — commentary through 2026 has pointed to a discount in the region of 30% below the 15-year historical average, alongside a market dividend yield sitting around the 5% mark. That combination — a real rally already underway, but valuations still below their own long-term norm — is the reason a fair amount of sell-side commentary has stayed constructive on the market rather than calling it overextended.

Banking has driven most of the move

The rally hasn't been evenly spread. Banking stocks have been the standout performers, and the reasons make sense on their own terms: lower interest rates are expected to boost loan book growth as borrowing becomes more attractive to businesses and households, asset quality has been improving as the broader economy stabilises, and Tier-1 banks in particular have been benefiting from SME lending recovery and continued digital transformation that's lowering their cost-to-income ratios over time. We've covered the individual banks in more depth in our banking sector deep dive, but the sector-level story is straightforward: cheaper money plus improving credit quality is about as favourable a setup as banking stocks get.

Lower rates help banks twice over — cheaper funding costs support margins today, and a more active lending environment supports loan book growth tomorrow. That double benefit is a big part of why banking has led this rally.

Telecoms and Safaricom's outsized influence

Safaricom's sheer size means the NSE's overall performance can't really be separated from Safaricom's own performance — it represents a dominant share of total market capitalisation, and continued growth in M-Pesa, data consumption and the broader push toward 5G rollout have kept the stock, and by extension a large chunk of the index, moving in a consistently positive direction. We've gone through the specifics of Safaricom's own numbers in a separate deep dive.

Consumer staples: steady, unglamorous, still working

Tobacco, brewing and other consumer staples names have continued to deliver the kind of defensive, consistent performance they're known for. These aren't the stocks generating headlines during a rally, but their earnings stability and reliable dividend payments — a topic we cover in more depth in our dividend investing piece — have kept them relevant to income-focused portfolios even while faster-moving sectors like banking grab more attention.

What could go wrong from here

Two things sit at the top of most analysts' watch lists. First, 2027 carries election risk — Kenyan election cycles have historically introduced periods of market uncertainty and reduced foreign investor appetite in the run-up, and 2026 sits close enough to that calendar that some of the caution is already starting to show up in longer-term positioning commentary, even amid the current rally. Second, global headwinds — shifts in US interest rate policy, global risk appetite, and commodity price swings — all filter through to a market like the NSE via foreign portfolio flows, regardless of how well the domestic economic story is playing out.

Neither of these is a reason to avoid the market. Both are reasons to size positions sensibly and not assume the current pace of gains is a permanent state of affairs rather than a favourable but ultimately cyclical macro backdrop.

How we'd think about positioning

A rational read of where things stand: the easing cycle and improving credit conditions have provided real, fundamentals-based support for the rally so far, not pure sentiment. Valuations, while no longer at the deeply depressed levels of a couple of years ago, still sit below the market's own long-run average, which leaves room for further re-rating if the macro backdrop continues to cooperate. A sensible approach for most investors is a diversified core across banking, telecoms and consumer staples — the three sectors that have driven and continue to anchor this market — while keeping some awareness of the 2027 election calendar as a factor that could introduce volatility well before the vote itself.
