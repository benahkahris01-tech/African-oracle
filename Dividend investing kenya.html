Dividend investing on the NSE:
who actually pays, and who disappoints

A headline yield of 12% looks fantastic on a spreadsheet. Whether it survives the next three annual general meetings is a completely different question.

← Back to all articles
May 2026 · The African Oracle Research Team · NSE Kenya

Dividend investing has a particular appeal on the NSE that it doesn't always have elsewhere. Capital gains on Kenyan shares are not taxed, dividends are only subject to a modest 5% withholding tax at source for resident shareholders — one of the lowest dividend tax rates of any major African exchange — and the market has a genuine bench of companies that have paid out cash, year after year, for decades. For a lot of Kenyan investors, particularly those closer to retirement or simply looking for income they can actually spend, this is the whole game.

But "high yield" and "good dividend stock" are not the same thing, and conflating them is how people end up disappointed. This piece walks through what actually makes a dividend sustainable, which NSE names have earned a reputation for reliability, and where the yield trap sits.

Yield is a symptom, not a strategy

A dividend yield is simply the annual dividend divided by the current share price. That means yield can go up for two very different reasons — the company raised its payout, or the share price fell. The market doesn't distinguish between these in the headline number, but you absolutely should.

A stock whose price has been sliding because the underlying business is struggling will often show up near the top of a "highest yield" screen, right before management is forced to cut the dividend and the yield collapses back down to something ordinary. This is the yield trap, and it catches income investors on every market in the world, not just the NSE.

Before you buy anything for its yield, ask why the yield is that high. Sometimes it's because the company is unusually generous and well-run. Sometimes it's the market quietly telling you the current payout won't last.

The names with genuinely long dividend track records

Some NSE-listed companies have built real reputations as dependable payers. Safaricom is usually the first one mentioned, and for good reason — it has never missed a dividend since listing in 2008, and M-Pesa's cash generation gives it an unusually predictable earnings base to pay out from. British American Tobacco Kenya is another regular fixture near the top of yield tables, typically paying out close to all of its earnings each year — tobacco businesses globally tend to run this way, prioritising cash return over reinvestment given the structural decline in smoking volumes. KenGen, Standard Chartered Bank Kenya and Co-operative Bank have also featured consistently among the higher-yielding, better-covered dividend payers on the exchange in recent periods.

Notice that Safaricom sits at the lower end of that yield range despite being the name most associated with dividend reliability. That's the trade-off in a nutshell: the more predictable and higher-quality the earnings stream, the more the market is usually willing to pay for the shares, which mechanically compresses the yield. The very high yields tend to sit with either genuinely cash-generative but slower-growth businesses like tobacco and utilities, or with stocks the market is pricing more cautiously.

What actually makes a dividend sustainable

A few things are worth checking before you trust any yield figure:

  • The payout ratio. This is dividends paid as a percentage of net profit. A company paying out 40–60% of earnings has room to keep the dividend flowing even in a weaker year. A company paying out 90% or more has very little buffer — one bad year and the board is choosing between cutting the dividend or borrowing to pay it.
  • Earnings trend, not just the last figure. A single strong year can flatter the payout ratio. Look at three to five years of earnings to see whether the business can actually support the payment through a cycle, not just at the peak of one.
  • Cash flow versus accounting profit. Profit on paper and cash in the bank are not the same thing, particularly for capital-intensive businesses. A company can report a healthy profit while struggling to generate the free cash flow needed to fund the dividend, especially if it's carrying heavy capital expenditure or working capital needs.
  • Sector cyclicality. Banks, insurers and telecoms tend to have more predictable earnings than manufacturers, agricultural counters, or commodity-linked names, where a single bad harvest or a swing in global prices can wipe out a year's profit and force a dividend cut with very little warning.

Sectors worth paying attention to

Banking dominates the high-yield end of the NSE, and for structural reasons that make sense — Kenyan banks have historically operated with strong net interest margins, disciplined cost control in the better-run names, and regulatory capital requirements that force a certain level of financial discipline. That said, banks are also the sector most exposed to credit cycles, so payout consistency can shift quickly if asset quality deteriorates.

Consumer staples — tobacco, brewing, and to some extent tea and agricultural processors — tend to offer more defensive, if less exciting, dividend profiles. People keep buying beer and cigarettes through a downturn in a way they don't necessarily keep buying discretionary goods, which supports earnings stability even when the broader economy is soft.

Utilities and power generation names like KenGen sit somewhere in between — regulated revenue provides a degree of predictability, but large capital projects and government policy shifts can affect the payout in ways that are harder to forecast from the outside.

Building an actual income portfolio

The mistake most beginner dividend investors make is screening for the single highest yield and buying that. A more durable approach spreads across a handful of names with different underlying drivers — a bank, a consumer staple, a telecom, maybe a REIT for genuine diversification — so that a dividend cut at any single company doesn't gut your overall income. Reinvesting dividends during the early years, rather than spending them immediately, also compounds the position size over time in a way that materially changes the outcome a decade out.

It's also worth remembering that NSE dividends are typically paid annually or semi-annually rather than quarterly, which is a genuine difference from US markets that catches some first-time investors off guard when they're trying to plan monthly cash flow around it.
