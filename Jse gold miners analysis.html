JSE gold miners:
GFI, ANG and HAR compared

Gold has had an extraordinary run. All three miners are benefiting. But rising costs, different balance sheets and very different geographic footprints mean they are not benefiting equally.

← Back to all articles
May 2026 · The African Oracle Research Team · JSE South Africa

Gold has had one of its strongest runs in decades, pushing well past previous records and dragging every JSE-listed gold miner along with it — share prices across the sector have moved dramatically higher over the past year. When a commodity moves like that, it's tempting to treat the miners that produce it as interchangeable ways to get exposure. They're not. Gold Fields, AngloGold Ashanti and Harmony Gold are exposed to the same gold price, but they run genuinely different businesses with different cost structures, different balance sheets, and very different geographic footprints — and those differences matter more, not less, once a boom starts to mature.

Where each company actually operates

This is the starting point, because it explains almost everything else about how these three compare. AngloGold Ashanti sold its last South African mining assets to Harmony back in 2020 and today operates across Africa, the Americas and Australia — a genuinely global portfolio with no domestic South African production exposure at all. Gold Fields operates a similarly international footprint spanning Ghana, Peru, Australia and Chile, with just one South African asset remaining — South Deep, a large, mechanised underground operation. Harmony Gold is the outlier: it remains the most South Africa-concentrated of the three, running a portfolio of underground and surface operations domestically alongside international assets in Papua New Guinea.

That concentration difference matters for a very specific reason — South African deep-level underground mining carries structurally higher costs, more complex labour dynamics, and greater exposure to domestic issues like electricity supply reliability, than the shallower, more mechanised operations typical of Gold Fields and AngloGold's newer international assets.

Cost structure: the number that actually decides who wins

In a gold bull market, the metric that separates the winners is not revenue — everyone's revenue is rising with the gold price — it's cost. The lower a miner's all-in sustaining cost (AISC) per ounce, the more of each dollar of rising gold price falls straight through to profit rather than being eaten by production costs.

What's notable across the whole sector in 2026 is that costs have been rising meaningfully — driven by higher royalty payments (royalties are typically a percentage of revenue, so they rise automatically as the gold price rises), stronger local currencies against the dollar in South Africa and Australia, and general inflation in labour, fuel and consumables. This is the dynamic worth understanding: even with gold near record highs, unit margins don't expand one-for-one with the gold price, because costs are climbing too.

A rising gold price lifts every miner's revenue. It does not lift every miner's margin by the same amount. Cost structure is what decides which one actually gets richer fastest.

Balance sheet strength: who can weather a pullback

Gold prices don't go up forever, and the miners best positioned to keep paying dividends and funding growth through an eventual pullback are the ones with the strongest balance sheets today, while the good times are still rolling.

Gold Fields has kept its net debt to adjusted EBITDA ratio at a conservative level — comfortably under 0.3x through much of 2026 — even after funding a substantial dividend payment, which reflects genuinely strong free cash flow generation from the business. AngloGold Ashanti has also built a considerably stronger balance sheet in recent years, generating a large jump in free cash flow and ending recent periods with several billion dollars of available liquidity. Harmony has likewise reported a low net debt to EBITDA ratio and healthy available liquidity. Across the board, the sector has used the current gold price windfall to genuinely de-lever rather than over-extend — which is the encouraging, disciplined version of a commodity boom, in contrast to the debt-fuelled excess that characterised parts of the sector during the previous gold cycle around 2011.

Growth pipeline: where the next few years of production come from

Gold Fields has been ramping up its Salares Norte mine in Chile, which delivered a substantial year-on-year production increase and is a genuinely important new growth leg for the company, alongside its longer-dated Windfall project in Canada targeting first gold toward the end of the decade. AngloGold Ashanti's growth is more spread across its existing African and Americas operations, including its Obuasi and Geita assets, without one single standout project dominating the growth story in the way Salares Norte does for Gold Fields. Harmony's growth is more domestically weighted, tied to extending the life of its existing South African underground operations and its international assets in Papua New Guinea.

Earnings predictability — the least obvious but arguably most important difference

South African deep-level underground mining is inherently less predictable quarter to quarter than shallower, more mechanised international operations — geotechnical issues, safety stoppages, and grade variability tend to hit underground operations harder. That makes Harmony's earnings, given its heavier South African weighting, somewhat more prone to the kind of operational surprise that can rattle a quarter's results, compared to Gold Fields and AngloGold, whose more geographically diversified, increasingly open-pit and mechanised portfolios tend to smooth out single-asset disruptions across a broader base.

This doesn't make Harmony a worse investment — a business more exposed to South African underground mining can also offer more direct leverage to any recovery in domestic mining conditions, and it has genuinely strengthened its own balance sheet and cost discipline in recent years. It does mean an investor should expect more earnings volatility from Harmony quarter to quarter than from the other two.

Putting it together

For an investor wanting the most internationally diversified, lower single-asset-risk exposure to gold, Gold Fields and AngloGold Ashanti's geographically spread portfolios are the more conservative choice, with Gold Fields offering a particularly clear near-term growth story through Salares Norte's ramp-up. For an investor who specifically wants leverage to South African gold mining conditions — and is comfortable with the higher earnings variability that comes with deep-level underground operations — Harmony offers the most direct exposure of the three, alongside a materially strengthened balance sheet compared to where the company sat a decade ago.

None of the three is a "safe" investment in the way a defensive consumer staple might be — gold miners are ultimately a leveraged bet on a commodity price that can and does reverse. But within that category, the differences in cost structure, balance sheet strength and geographic concentration between these three names are large enough that treating them as interchangeable would be a genuine mistake.
