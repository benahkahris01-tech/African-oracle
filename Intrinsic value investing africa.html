Does value investing
actually work here?

Ben Graham and Warren Buffett built their reputations in the world's deepest, most-followed market. Whether the same logic holds up in a thinner, less-analysed market like the NSE is worth actually thinking through, not assuming.

← Back to all articles
May 2026 · The African Oracle Research Team · Strategy

Intrinsic value investing rests on a simple premise: every business has some underlying worth, based on the cash it can be reasonably expected to generate over its lifetime, and the market price of its shares sometimes drifts away from that underlying worth — sometimes above it, sometimes below. The investor's job is to estimate that intrinsic worth independently, then buy when the market price sits meaningfully below it and hold, or sell, when it doesn't.

It's a framework built and refined almost entirely in the US market, by investors with access to decades of data, armies of analysts checking each other's work, and some of the deepest, most liquid trading in the world. None of those conditions describe the NSE particularly well. So does the framework still hold up? We think the core logic does — but the practical execution needs real adjustment.

Why the core logic should still apply

The basic premise of value investing doesn't actually depend on market depth or analyst coverage — it depends on markets being made up of people, and people being prone to overreaction, underreaction, and inattention. If anything, a market with fewer analysts watching it and less liquidity is a market where mispricing has more room to persist, simply because there are fewer participants actively working to correct it. In a deeply efficient market like the US large-cap space, a genuine mispricing tends to get arbitraged away quickly once spotted. On the NSE, a mispricing can sit there for a long stretch of time before enough capital and attention arrives to close the gap — which cuts both ways: it's an opportunity for a patient investor, and a real risk that a "cheap" stock stays cheap for far longer than textbook value investing theory would suggest is reasonable.

Less-followed markets don't make value investing easier. They make the opportunities potentially larger and the patience required considerably longer.

Where the practical challenges actually sit

Data quality and availability. US value investors can pull decades of clean, standardised financial data with a few clicks. On the NSE, historical data is thinner, less standardised across companies, and harder to access in one place — part of the reason a resource like a dedicated screener matters more here than it might in a market where the data is commoditised and freely available everywhere.

Liquidity risk on the way out. Finding an undervalued NSE stock is only half the job. If the position is large relative to the stock's typical daily trading volume, exiting it — even once the market eventually recognises the value you identified — can itself move the price against you. This is a real, practical constraint that barely registers for a value investor working in large-cap US names, and it needs to factor into position sizing on the NSE.

Currency and macro noise. A genuinely undervalued Kenyan company can still see its share price languish, or fall further, because of currency weakness or a macro shock entirely unrelated to the business's own fundamentals. Distinguishing "the market is wrong about this specific business" from "the whole market is being repriced for macro reasons that have nothing to do with this business" is harder here, and getting it wrong is a common way value investors lose money even when their underlying analysis of the company itself was sound.

Corporate governance variance. Value investing assumes that if you buy a dollar of assets for fifty cents, you'll eventually realise something close to that dollar of value — through earnings, dividends, or an eventual re-rating. That assumption depends on management actually running the business in shareholders' interests. Governance standards vary more widely across NSE and JSE-listed companies than across, say, the S&P 500, and a "cheap" stock attached to a management team with a poor capital allocation or minority-shareholder track record can simply stay cheap indefinitely, or see that discount justified by genuine value destruction rather than closed by a re-rating.

What a sensible value approach looks like on the NSE and JSE

A few adjustments to the standard playbook make it considerably more workable here. Weighting more heavily toward balance sheet strength than a US value investor might — given the sharper consequences of leverage in a higher interest rate environment we cover in our piece on financial strength — provides more of a margin of safety against macro shocks specifically. Paying closer attention to management track record and governance history than pure valuation metrics alone would suggest, since the "value gets realised eventually" assumption depends heavily on management actually being aligned with shareholders. And sizing positions with real liquidity constraints in mind, rather than assuming you can exit a position as cleanly as the numbers on a spreadsheet imply.

Where this framework tends to work best in practice

Larger, more liquid NSE and JSE names — the kind covered in our sector deep dives on Equity Group and the wider banking sector — offer the closest analogue to how value investing typically works in developed markets, since liquidity and available information are closer to what the framework was originally built around. Smaller, more thinly traded counters can absolutely offer genuine value opportunities, but they demand considerably more patience, deeper individual company research, and real comfort with the liquidity risk on the way out.

The honest answer

Value investing isn't a strategy that stops working outside the US — the underlying logic of buying businesses for less than they're worth is universal. But the NSE and JSE demand a version of the discipline that leans more heavily on balance sheet strength, governance quality and realistic liquidity assessment than the standard developed-market playbook requires, and that rewards genuine patience over the kind of shorter re-rating windows investors in deeper markets sometimes get to enjoy.
