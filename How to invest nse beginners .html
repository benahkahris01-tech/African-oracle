How to buy your
first NSE share

No jargon, no assumed knowledge. Everything you actually need to go from "I've never bought a share" to owning a piece of a Kenyan company.

← Back to all articles
May 2026 · The African Oracle Research Team · Beginners

A lot of Kenyans grow up hearing that "the stock market" is something for wealthy people with a broker on speed dial. It isn't. You can open an account from your phone in under an hour, and you can start with an amount most people can genuinely afford. What actually stops most first-timers isn't money — it's not knowing the steps. So here they are, in order.

Step 1: Understand what you're actually opening — a CDS account

Before you can own a single share, you need a CDS account. CDS stands for Central Depository System, and it's essentially your electronic locker for shares — instead of paper share certificates, your holdings sit as digital records with the Central Depository and Settlement Corporation (CDSC), the body that holds and tracks share ownership on the NSE. You cannot buy or sell a listed Kenyan share without one.

Here's a detail worth knowing and genuinely reassuring: your CDS account sits with the CDSC, not with your stockbroker. Your broker is the intermediary who executes trades on your behalf, but your shares are recorded independently of them. If your broker ran into financial trouble, your shareholding itself wouldn't be at risk in the way cash sitting in a normal account might be.

Step 2: Choose a stockbroker

In Kenya you cannot buy shares directly from the exchange — everyone goes through a licensed stockbroker or investment bank, all of which are regulated by the Capital Markets Authority (CMA). There's a genuine mix of options: established names with branch networks and phone-based support for people who want a human to talk to, and increasingly popular mobile-first apps that let you open an account and start trading entirely from your phone, often within the same day.

What to actually look for when comparing brokers:

  • Minimum opening amount — this varies significantly between providers, from a few hundred shillings on some mobile apps to higher minimums at traditional brokers.
  • Brokerage commission — typically somewhere in the 1.5% to 2.1% range per trade across most licensed brokers, charged on both buying and selling.
  • Platform quality — whether you can realistically place an order, check your holdings, and see live prices from your phone without frustration.
  • CMA licensing — non-negotiable. The CMA publishes a list of licensed brokers on its website; never send money to anyone claiming to trade NSE shares who isn't on that list.

Step 3: Gather your documents and open the account

You'll typically need your national ID or passport, your KRA PIN, a passport-sized photo, and your bank account details for receiving dividends and withdrawals. Most brokers now offer fully digital onboarding — you photograph your documents, verify your identity, and submit the application from your phone. Approval commonly takes anywhere from same-day to a few working days depending on the broker and whether you applied digitally or through a physical branch. Once approved, you'll receive a unique CDS account number by SMS or email — keep this safe, as it's your permanent identifier for all future share transactions across every company you ever invest in.

A valid KRA PIN is mandatory. There's no way around this step — it's a compliance requirement, not an optional extra some brokers happen to ask for.

Step 4: Fund your account

Once your CDS account and broker trading account are active, you fund it — usually via M-Pesa or a bank transfer directly to your broker. This is separate from opening the account itself, and you'll generally see the funds reflected in your trading account within minutes for mobile money transfers, a little longer for bank transfers.

Step 5: Place your first order

With funds in your account, you can place an order — either through your broker's app, their online trading platform, or by phone or in person for the more traditional providers. You'll choose the stock, the number of shares (most counters trade in board lots, commonly 100 shares, though this varies by counter and platform), and either a market order — buy at the current price — or a limit order, where you specify the maximum price you're willing to pay and the order only fills if the market reaches it.

NSE trading hours run 9:30am to 3:00pm, Monday to Friday. Orders placed outside those hours simply queue for the next trading session. Settlement — the point at which the shares are officially yours and the cash has left your account — typically completes within a few business days of the trade, following the standard T+3 cycle common across most exchanges.

What it actually costs

Typical Costs When Buying NSE Shares
Brokerage commission ................ ~1.5%–2.1% of transaction value
CMA levy ............................. 0.14%
NSE transaction fee .................. ~0.0036%
CDSC fee .............................. flat fee per transaction (varies by broker)
Capital gains tax .................... none on individual share trading gains
Dividend withholding tax ............. 5% for resident individual shareholders

Fee structures vary between brokers and can change — confirm current rates with your chosen broker before trading.

The absence of capital gains tax on individual NSE share trading is a genuinely useful feature of investing in Kenya compared to many other markets, and it's worth knowing about upfront rather than discovering it later.

What to actually buy as a first share

This is where most beginner guides either give bad advice or dodge the question entirely. We won't tell you exactly what to buy — that depends on your own goals, risk appetite and time horizon. But a sensible starting principle is to begin with large, liquid, well-understood companies rather than smaller, thinly traded counters. A company like Safaricom, Equity Group, or one of the larger banks gives you the benefit of easier trading, more available information, and a track record you can actually research, while you're still learning how the market and your own emotions around it behave. You can branch into smaller, less liquid counters once you've got a few trading cycles of experience behind you.

A realistic first-timer mistake to avoid

The single most common mistake new NSE investors make isn't picking the wrong stock — it's checking the price every single day and reacting emotionally to short-term moves. Share prices move constantly for reasons that have nothing to do with the underlying value of the business you own. Decide on your investment thesis before you buy, write it down if it helps, and give it real time — years, not weeks — to play out before judging whether it worked.
