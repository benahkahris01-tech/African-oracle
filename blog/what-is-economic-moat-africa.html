Economic moats:
why they matter more here than anywhere

In a market with fewer analysts, thinner liquidity and more volatile earnings, a genuine competitive moat is worth even more than it is in London or New York. Here's how to actually find one.

← Back to all articles
May 2026 · The African Oracle Research Team · Education

The term "economic moat" was popularised by Warren Buffett to describe a business with a durable competitive advantage — something that protects its profits from competitors the way a moat protects a castle. It's one of those ideas that sounds simple in a shareholder letter and turns out to be genuinely difficult to apply in practice, because the whole point is spotting it before everyone else does.

On the NSE and JSE, moats matter arguably more than they do in a market like the US. Fewer analysts cover these companies. Liquidity is thinner. Earnings can swing more with currency and macro shocks. In that kind of environment, a business that can defend its pricing and market share year after year is worth a real premium, because it's one of the few things you can actually count on holding steady when everything else around it is moving.

The four basic types of moat

Moats generally come from one of a handful of sources, and it's worth knowing which one you're looking at because they hold up differently under pressure.

  • Network effects. The product gets more valuable as more people use it. Every additional M-Pesa user makes M-Pesa more useful to every existing user, because there are more people to send money to and receive money from. This kind of moat tends to be the hardest to dislodge once it's established.
  • Switching costs. It's expensive, risky, or simply annoying for a customer to leave. A bank that holds your salary account, your standing orders, your loan history and your credit score has switching costs working in its favour even if a competitor offers a marginally better savings rate.
  • Cost advantages. The company can produce or distribute at a lower cost than anyone else, usually through scale, and can therefore undercut competitors while still protecting margins.
  • Intangible assets. Brand strength, licences, or regulatory approvals that are difficult or impossible for a new entrant to replicate. A telecoms spectrum licence or a brewing license in a market with strict regulation functions as a genuine barrier to entry.

Safaricom: about as clear a moat as you'll find on the NSE

Safaricom is the textbook case, and it's worth understanding exactly why. M-Pesa isn't just a mobile money product — it's a network that touches the vast majority of Kenyan adults, integrated into how salaries are paid, how small businesses take payment, how school fees get settled, and how people move money to family upcountry. That's a network effect moat layered on top of genuine switching costs, since so much of a typical Kenyan's financial life runs through it.

You can see the moat show up directly in the numbers. Safaricom has consistently posted return on equity well above 30% and net margins in the low-to-mid 20% range — figures that would be considered exceptional for a telecom operator almost anywhere in the world, developed or emerging. That's not an accident of good management alone. It's what a genuine, hard-to-replicate moat looks like when it shows up on a set of financial statements.

A moat isn't a story you tell about a company. It's something you should be able to see directly in sustained, above-average returns on capital that competitors haven't been able to compete away.

Where moats are less obvious, and more debatable

Most NSE and JSE companies don't have a moat anywhere near as clean as Safaricom's, and that's completely normal — most businesses anywhere in the world don't. The more interesting, and more useful, exercise is looking at the borderline cases.

Take East African banking. Equity Group has built something closer to a genuine moat than most of its peers, through a distribution network, brand trust built over two decades, and a low-cost deposit base that gives it a real cost-of-funds advantage. But banking as a sector is more contestable than people assume — customers do switch when a competitor offers materially better digital experience or pricing, and fintech disruption is a live, ongoing threat to traditional banking moats across the continent, not a hypothetical one.

Brewing and consumer staples occupy an interesting middle ground. A dominant beer or spirits brand benefits from real distribution advantages and brand loyalty that's genuinely hard to displace quickly. But it's not impregnable — a well-funded challenger with a strong marketing push and the right price point can chip away at share over a five-to-ten-year horizon in a way that would be much harder against something like M-Pesa's network effect.

The trap: mistaking "well-known" for "moated"

The most common mistake investors make with this concept is assuming any recognisable, market-leading brand automatically has a moat. Recognition is not the same as protection. A company can be extremely well known and still face real competitive pressure that erodes its margins over time — plenty of once-dominant African retailers and manufacturers have discovered this the hard way when a leaner, better-capitalised competitor entered their market.

The honest test is this: if a well-funded competitor entered tomorrow with a genuinely good product, could they take meaningful market share within three to five years? If the answer is clearly no, you're probably looking at a real moat. If the answer is "maybe, if they executed well," you're looking at a business with some advantages, but not the kind of durable protection that deserves a premium valuation on that basis alone.

Why this matters for how you actually invest

A wide-moat business is one you can hold through a rough macro patch with genuine conviction, because you understand exactly why its competitive position should survive a downturn intact. A narrow or non-existent moat means you're really betting on execution and market conditions staying favourable — which is a perfectly valid bet, but a different and generally riskier one, and it deserves a different sizing in a portfolio and a lower price to justify the risk.

Neither approach is wrong. But conflating the two — paying a wide-moat premium for a business that doesn't actually have one — is one of the more expensive mistakes an investor can make, and it's a mistake that's easy to make on markets where fewer independent analysts are checking the story for you.
