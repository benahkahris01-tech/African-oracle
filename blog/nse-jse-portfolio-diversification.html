Building a portfolio
across the NSE and JSE

Most Kenyan investors hold only Kenyan shares. Most South African investors hold only South African shares. Both are carrying more single-country risk than they probably realise.

← Back to all articles
May 2026 · The African Oracle Research Team · Strategy

Ask most Kenyan retail investors what "diversification" means and they'll usually describe spreading money across a few different NSE sectors — a bank, a telecom, maybe a consumer staple. That's genuine diversification against company-specific risk. It does almost nothing against country-specific risk, because every one of those holdings is still exposed to the same Kenyan shilling, the same Kenyan interest rate cycle, the same Kenyan political calendar, and the same Kenyan macro shocks. The reverse is just as true for South African investors sitting entirely in JSE names.

Why single-country concentration is a real risk, not a theoretical one

A portfolio of ten different NSE stocks across five different sectors looks diversified on paper. But if a shilling devaluation, a specific Kenyan policy shock, or a domestic political event hits sentiment broadly, most of those ten positions can move together, in the same direction, at the same time — because the thing actually driving the correlation isn't the sector, it's the country. We saw a version of this dynamic in reverse during the current NSE rally we cover in our 2026 market outlook — a single macro driver, the easing cycle, lifted banking, telecoms and consumer names together, which is exactly the same mechanism that can work in the opposite direction during a downturn.

Sector diversification protects you if one company or one industry has a bad year. Country diversification protects you if the country has a bad year. Most African retail portfolios only have the first kind.

What NSE and JSE actually offer each other

We went through the mechanics of crossing between the two markets in detail in our JSE versus NSE comparison — currency exposure, the withholding tax gap, settlement and account-opening friction. The diversification case sits on top of those mechanics: the two markets are exposed to genuinely different economic drivers. Kenya's economy leans more heavily on agriculture, telecoms-led financial inclusion, and East African regional trade. South Africa's economy carries far more weight in mining, heavy industry, and global commodity cycles, alongside deeper integration with global capital markets generally. A shock to global commodity prices hits the JSE more directly than the NSE. A shock to East African agricultural output or regional trade hits the NSE more directly than the JSE. Holding meaningful positions in both markets means a shock concentrated in one economic driver doesn't automatically hit your entire portfolio at once.

Sector access is the other half of the case, which we touched on in the JSE-versus-NSE piece: the JSE offers deep exposure to global mining and resources, luxury goods, and internationally diversified industrials that simply don't exist in meaningful size on the NSE. A Kenyan investor's portfolio is almost always heavily weighted toward banking, telecoms and consumer staples by default, purely because that's what's available domestically in scale — not necessarily because that's the ideal sector mix for their goals.

A practical way to think about allocation

There's no universal correct split — it depends on your own risk tolerance, how much currency risk you're comfortable carrying, and how much effort you want to put into managing a second market's tax and account mechanics. But a few principles are worth applying regardless of the exact numbers you land on:

  • Keep the core at home first. Your local market is where you have the deepest information advantage, the lowest transaction friction, and — for most people — the clearest understanding of the specific companies involved. A cross-border allocation should generally supplement a solid domestic core, not replace it.
  • Size the cross-border allocation to your currency comfort. Since currency movement adds a real layer of volatility on top of the underlying stock performance, a first cross-border allocation is usually more sensible as a modest portion of the overall portfolio rather than a large bet, particularly for an investor doing this for the first time.
  • Start with large, liquid, well-understood names. Just as we suggested for NSE beginners in our step-by-step investing guide, the same logic applies doubly when investing in an unfamiliar market — large, well-covered JSE blue chips are easier to research and trade out of than smaller, thinly followed counters.
  • Account for the after-tax picture. The dividend withholding tax gap between the two markets — 5% in Kenya versus a standard 20% in South Africa — means a cross-border yield comparison needs an after-tax adjustment to be meaningful, a point we detailed in our JSE dividend stocks article.

What this looks like for different investor profiles

A Kenyan investor already holding a diversified NSE book of banks, telecoms and consumer names might look at JSE mining and resources names specifically, since that's the sector gap that's largest and least replicable at home — and it happens to pair well with a portfolio otherwise concentrated in financials and consumer staples, which tend to respond to different economic conditions than commodity producers.

A South African investor holding a JSE-heavy portfolio, by contrast, might find more value looking toward Kenyan financials and telecoms specifically for the reverse reason — genuine exposure to East African financial inclusion and mobile money growth, a theme with limited direct JSE equivalent, and one we've explored in depth in our Safaricom analysis.

The honest trade-off

Cross-border African diversification is more effort than staying entirely in your home market — more account complexity, more tax nuance, more currency risk to actively manage rather than ignore. It's not a strategy for someone who wants the absolute simplest possible portfolio. But for an investor with a reasonably established domestic core already in place, it's one of the more genuine ways to reduce single-country risk that a lot of African retail investors currently carry without necessarily realising it, simply because "diversified" in their head has meant sector diversification within one economy, rather than diversification across economies entirely.
