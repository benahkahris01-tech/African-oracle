High-yield JSE stocks:
genuine income versus yield traps

A 9% yield can mean a well-run, cash-generative business rewarding shareholders. It can also mean the market is pricing in a dividend cut. Here's how to tell which is which.

← Back to all articles
May 2026 · The African Oracle Research Team · JSE South Africa

The JSE has a genuinely deep bench of dividend-paying companies, spanning banking, insurance, resources and diversified industrials — a much broader set of high-yielding sectors than the NSE can offer, which we touched on in our JSE versus NSE comparison. But breadth cuts both ways. A bigger universe of high-yield stocks also means a bigger universe of yield traps, and South Africa's resource-heavy market has produced some particularly instructive examples of dividends that looked great right up until they weren't.

The banks and insurers: the more dependable end of the spectrum

South Africa's major banks and insurers have generally been the more reliable dividend payers on the exchange, for reasons that mirror what we've seen work in Kenyan banking — regulated capital requirements, disciplined provisioning, and business models that generate genuinely predictable cash flow through most of a normal economic cycle.

Standard Bank in particular stands out for a payout ratio that's sat around the mid-50% range in recent periods — comfortably covered by earnings, with room to keep growing the dividend if profits continue climbing, which is exactly the profile you want to see behind a high yield rather than a payout ratio pushed up near 90% or beyond, where there's no real margin for error.

Sanlam and the insurance sector

Sanlam represents a slightly different flavour of dividend stock — a diversified financial services and insurance group with a lower headline yield than the banks, but a payout ratio under 50% that leaves genuine room for the dividend to keep growing alongside earnings, which it has done at a reasonable pace in recent years. Insurance businesses in general tend to offer more gradual, less headline-grabbing dividend growth than banks, but with a comparably defensive earnings base underneath it.

The cautionary tale: resources and commodity cyclicality

This is where the JSE teaches its most important dividend lesson, and Sasol is the textbook case. For years, Sasol was a fixture on lists of top JSE dividend payers, backed by strong cash generation from its chemicals and synthetic fuels business. Then the combination of a collapsing oil price, heavy debt taken on for a major US chemicals project, and broader commodity cycle pressure forced the company to suspend its dividend entirely for a period — wiping out what had looked, right up until it happened, like one of the more dependable income stocks on the exchange.

Sasol didn't just cut its dividend once — it went from a market favourite for income investors to paying nothing at all, inside a relatively short window. That's the risk sitting underneath every commodity-linked "high yield" stock, and it's not a hypothetical.

The lesson isn't "avoid resources stocks" — commodity businesses can be excellent dividend payers during the right part of the cycle, as gold miners have demonstrated during the recent gold price surge we cover in our gold miners comparison. The lesson is that commodity-linked dividends are inherently more cyclical and less predictable than dividends from a bank, an insurer, or a consumer staples business, and they need to be sized and monitored differently in a portfolio. A high yield from a cyclical commodity business is compensation for that cyclicality risk, not a free lunch.

What actually separates sustainable from fragile

  • Payout ratio relative to earnings. A ratio comfortably under 60% generally leaves room to weather a weaker year without cutting. Anything pushing toward, or past, 90% has very little buffer.
  • Cash flow coverage, not just accounting profit. A dividend needs to be paid in actual cash. Businesses with heavy capital expenditure requirements — mining and resources chief among them — can show a healthy accounting profit while genuinely struggling to fund both reinvestment and the dividend from free cash flow.
  • Debt levels. A company carrying significant debt is far more exposed to a downturn forcing a choice between servicing debt and paying shareholders. We go into this in detail in our piece on financial strength, and it's arguably the single most important check before trusting any high yield.
  • Sector cyclicality. Banks, insurers and consumer staples generally offer more predictable payout histories than mining, energy or heavy industrials, where a single commodity price cycle can force a rapid reversal regardless of how well the company is otherwise managed.

Currency and tax, for Kenyan or other non-resident investors

Worth repeating from our JSE-versus-NSE piece: South Africa's dividend withholding tax sits at a standard 20%, considerably higher than Kenya's 5%, though a reduced rate may apply for investors from countries with an applicable double taxation agreement with South Africa. Factor that into any yield comparison against NSE names — the headline number needs an after-tax adjustment to be genuinely comparable.

Building a JSE income allocation that actually holds up

A sensible approach spreads across sectors with genuinely different earnings drivers — a bank or two, an insurer, perhaps a defensive consumer name — rather than concentrating in whichever names currently show the single highest yield on a screener. The resources sector can absolutely feature, given how strong dividend flows have been from gold miners during the current price cycle, but it deserves a smaller, clearly-understood allocation given its cyclicality, rather than forming the core of an income portfolio the way a bank or insurer reasonably can.
