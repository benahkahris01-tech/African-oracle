Financial strength:
why debt matters more here

A debt-to-equity ratio that would barely raise an eyebrow in Frankfurt can be a genuine warning sign in Nairobi. The reason comes down to one number: the cost of borrowing.

← Back to all articles
May 2026 · The African Oracle Research Team · Education

Debt gets treated as a fairly generic risk factor in most investing guides — "too much debt is bad, check the balance sheet." That's true as far as it goes, but it misses something specific to markets like ours: the same debt-to-equity ratio can represent a completely different level of actual risk depending on where the company is borrowing, and at what rate.

The number that changes everything: cost of borrowing

Kenya's Central Bank Rate has sat in the region of 9% through 2026, on the back of an easing cycle from higher levels in prior years. South Africa's repo rate has moved in a broadly similar band, sitting around 6.75–7% through much of 2026 after its own series of cuts and a brief hike. Compare that to major developed economies, where policy rates have often sat meaningfully lower through long stretches of the past decade. The gap compounds once you get to actual corporate borrowing rates — a Kenyan company financing through a local bank is typically paying a rate that includes the base rate plus a margin, often landing well into double digits once you account for the risk premium banks attach to corporate lending.

A company carrying debt at 14% interest needs to earn a meaningfully higher return on that borrowed capital just to break even on the decision to borrow it, compared to a company paying 4% somewhere else. The exact same debt level is a different risk entirely.

This is the core reason a "reasonable" debt-to-equity ratio in a European or American context can be considerably more dangerous for a Kenyan or South African company. The interest bill eats a larger share of operating profit at a higher rate, leaving a thinner buffer before a revenue shock turns into a genuine solvency problem.

The metrics that actually matter

Debt-to-equity ratio is the starting point — total debt divided by shareholder equity. It tells you how much of the company is financed by borrowing versus by shareholders' own capital. A ratio under 1.0 generally suggests the company owns more than it owes; well above that, and the business is leaning heavily on borrowed money to fund its operations and growth.

Interest cover — operating profit divided by interest expense — is arguably more useful than debt-to-equity on its own, because it directly answers the question that actually matters: can this company comfortably service what it owes out of its current earnings? A ratio below roughly 3x is generally considered a warning sign; well above that, and the company has real breathing room even if earnings dip for a year or two.

Currency composition of debt is a distinctly African-market consideration that barely registers as a risk factor in developed-market analysis. A Kenyan or South African company that has borrowed in dollars to fund local operations is exposed to currency risk on top of ordinary business risk — if the shilling or rand weakens, the local-currency cost of servicing that dollar debt rises even if nothing about the underlying business has changed. This has caught out more than one African corporate borrower over the years, and it's worth specifically checking a company's debt currency mix in its annual report rather than assuming it's irrelevant.

Debt maturity profile matters more in a higher-rate environment too. A company with a large tranche of debt coming up for refinancing in a period of elevated rates can see its interest costs jump meaningfully even without taking on any new borrowing, simply by rolling existing debt over at the new, higher prevailing rate.

Sector patterns worth knowing

Banks are a structural exception to most of this — a bank's core business is borrowing (deposits) and lending, so a "high" debt-to-equity ratio by ordinary corporate standards is completely normal for a bank and needs to be assessed using banking-specific metrics like capital adequacy ratios instead. Don't apply an industrial company's debt framework to a bank; it will give you a nonsensical answer.

Capital-intensive sectors — mining, telecoms infrastructure, power generation — carry structurally higher debt levels because the businesses require large upfront investment before they generate meaningful revenue. The question there isn't "is there debt" but whether the cash flow being generated is growing to comfortably service and eventually reduce it, which is exactly the dynamic we walked through with JSE gold miners' balance sheets in our gold miners comparison.

Consumer staples and asset-light service businesses generally carry, and should carry, less debt — there's less justification for heavy borrowing when the business doesn't require large fixed capital investment to operate and grow.

A real example of debt going wrong

Sasol's dividend suspension, which we cover in detail in our JSE dividend stocks piece, is as much a debt story as a commodity story. The company had taken on substantial debt to fund a major US chemicals project, and when the oil price collapsed at the same time, the combination of reduced cash generation and a heavy debt service burden left the board with no real choice but to cut shareholder distributions to preserve cash for debt obligations. That's the mechanism by which excessive leverage turns an ordinary commodity downturn into a genuine balance sheet crisis — the debt amplifies the damage from a revenue shock that a lower-geared competitor might have absorbed far more comfortably.

How to actually use this when screening stocks

Check debt-to-equity and interest cover together, not in isolation — a high debt ratio with strong interest cover is a very different situation from a high debt ratio with thin cover. Check the currency composition of debt for any company with meaningful borrowing, particularly in sectors like mining, aviation or manufacturing where dollar-denominated debt is common. And weigh the local cost of capital specifically — a Kenyan or South African company's "safe" debt threshold should generally sit lower than the rule-of-thumb figures you'll find in most generic, developed-market-focused investing content, precisely because the interest rate environment here is structurally higher.

Check debt-to-equity, interest cover and balance sheet strength for any NSE or JSE stock.
