Equity Group:
the most interesting bank in East Africa

Over 22 million customers, a balance sheet past KES 2 trillion, and regional subsidiaries now generating half the group's profit. What are you actually buying?

← Back to all articles
May 2026 · The African Oracle Research Team · NSE Kenya

We touched on Equity Group in our broader NSE banking sector piece, but the business deserves its own closer look. Equity isn't just another large Kenyan bank — it's arguably the clearest example on the exchange of a genuine transformation story: from a building society serving low-income Kenyan savers in the 1990s to a pan-African financial services group where, as of 2026, regional subsidiaries outside Kenya generate roughly half of total group banking profit.

The numbers behind the transformation

Equity's FY2025 results, announced in March 2026, were genuinely record-breaking for the group — profit after tax rose 55% to KES 75.5 billion, up from KES 48.8 billion the prior year. The balance sheet expanded 9% to KES 1.97 trillion, with net loans growing 8% to KES 882.5 billion. Momentum carried into 2026: Q1 profit after tax rose a further 24% year-on-year to KES 19.1 billion, with return on equity reported at a disciplined 22.6% and return on assets at 3.9% — both strong figures by regional banking standards.

The regional story is now the real story

This is the single most important shift to understand about Equity in 2026: it is no longer really a "Kenyan bank with some regional exposure." Regional subsidiaries now account for roughly half of group banking profitability and over half of total banking assets. The growth rates coming out of individual markets have been striking — EquityBCDC in the DRC grew profit after tax 58% to KES 24.7 billion for FY2025, Uganda's subsidiary profit jumped fivefold to KES 3.6 billion, and Tanzania grew profit 125%. Into Q1 2026, Tanzania kept accelerating with a 150% profit increase, Rwanda grew 36%, and the DRC business added a further 32%.

For years, Equity Bank Kenya was the engine pulling the rest of the group along. That's now genuinely reversed in places — several of the regional units are growing faster than the Kenyan core, and collectively they're just as important to group earnings as Kenya is.

The DRC deserves particular attention as the largest single regional contributor. It's a market with enormous under-penetration in formal banking — a genuine long-runway growth opportunity — but also one with real currency volatility and operational complexity. The consistency of strong DRC growth through both FY2025 and into Q1 2026 is an encouraging sign of execution, though it remains the kind of market that deserves ongoing monitoring rather than being assumed as a permanently safe growth driver.

Where the growth is actually coming from within Kenya

Even with regional units growing faster in percentage terms, Equity Bank Kenya itself remains far from a mature, slow-growth operation — Q1 2026 profit after tax rose 21% to KES 10.3 billion, and the bank maintained its leadership position in Kenyan MSME lending, disbursing over a third of all MSME loans issued nationwide in the quarter. Equity's long-standing focus on small and medium business lending — a segment many larger banks historically underserved — continues to be a genuine differentiator and a source of loyal, sticky customer relationships that reinforce the distribution and brand moat we discussed in our economic moat article.

Digital transformation is the other major driver worth understanding. The group has reported that the vast majority of transactions now happen outside a physical branch, with the large majority of those flowing through fully digital channels. That shift matters directly for profitability — digital transactions cost a fraction of what a branch-based transaction costs to process, and that operating leverage is a meaningful contributor to the margin improvement showing up in recent results.

Beyond banking: the insurance and non-banking businesses

Equity's insurance arm has also become a genuine growth contributor rather than a side business — gross written premiums rose 75% to KES 9.17 billion in FY2025, with profit before tax from insurance up 36%. Into Q1 2026, gross written premiums grew a further 30%, contributing to a 53% jump in insurance profit before tax. This diversification into insurance, alongside the group's broader push into non-banking financial services, reduces Equity's reliance on traditional lending margins alone and adds a second, faster-growing profit stream to the group.

The risks worth weighing

Regional concentration cuts both ways — the same diversification that's driving growth also means Equity's earnings are now meaningfully exposed to political and currency risk across seven different countries rather than concentrated in the comparatively more stable and familiar Kenyan market alone. A shock in any single large regional market, particularly the DRC given its scale within the group, would have a more material group-level impact today than it would have five years ago, before regional operations reached their current share of profitability.

Credit quality through a rate-cutting cycle is the other factor worth monitoring. Loan growth of around 8-9% alongside falling interest rates is generally a healthy combination, but it's worth watching whether asset quality holds up as the loan book expands, particularly across the faster-growing regional units where credit risk assessment infrastructure may be less mature than in the well-established Kenyan operation.

Where this leaves an investor

Equity in 2026 looks less like a traditional Kenyan bank and more like a genuinely diversified pan-African financial services group, with strong, broad-based growth across both its core Kenyan operations and an increasingly important regional footprint, alongside a fast-growing insurance business layered on top. Return on equity above 20% and consistent double-digit-to-triple-digit growth across multiple markets are the kind of numbers that justify the premium the market has generally attached to Equity relative to some of its NSE banking peers — the question for any investor is whether that regional diversification is being adequately rewarded at the current price, or whether the market has already priced in most of the good news the recent results have delivered.
