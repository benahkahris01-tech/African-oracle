Safaricom in 2026:
the moat is real — is the price?

Everyone in Kenya knows Safaricom. Fewer people have actually sat down and worked through whether today's share price reflects what the business is worth. We did.

← Back to all articles
May 2026 · The African Oracle Research Team · NSE Kenya

Safaricom is, without much argument, the most important company on the Nairobi Securities Exchange. It's the largest counter by market capitalisation, the most heavily traded stock on the exchange by a wide margin, and for a huge number of first-time Kenyan investors it's the first share they ever buy. Familiarity, though, is not the same thing as a sound investment case. This article works through what's actually driving the business in 2026, and whether the current valuation makes sense given where that business is headed.

What the latest numbers actually show

Safaricom's full-year results for FY2026 were genuinely strong. Group revenue came in at roughly KES 424 billion, up around 10% year on year, while net income jumped more sharply — up roughly 37% to about KES 95.6 billion. Earnings per share rose from KES 1.74 to KES 2.39. Net profit margin expanded from around 18% to roughly 23%, which is a meaningful improvement for a business of this size in a single year.

The margin expansion is the number worth sitting with, because it tells a specific story: this wasn't just a revenue growth story, it was a story about the business getting more profitable on each additional shilling of revenue it brought in. That kind of operating leverage is exactly what you'd expect from a business with the kind of network effect moat M-Pesa provides — once the infrastructure and user base exist, incremental revenue tends to fall through to the bottom line at a higher rate than the original revenue did.

Where the growth is actually coming from

M-Pesa remains the core of the investment case, but it's worth being specific about why. It isn't just a money transfer product anymore — it's evolved into a broader financial services platform, layering lending products, savings products and merchant payments on top of the original send-and-receive functionality. Every one of those layers adds a new, higher-margin revenue stream on top of infrastructure Safaricom has already built and largely paid for. That's a genuinely attractive economic structure: the marginal cost of adding a new financial product to an existing user base is far lower than the cost of building a telecoms network from scratch.

Data and mobile broadband continue to grow as smartphone penetration rises across Kenya, and this is a straightforward secular tailwind that doesn't require heroic assumptions — more Kenyans get smartphones every year, and that group needs data. Voice revenue, the original business, is a much smaller part of the growth story at this point and functions more as a stable, slow-declining base than a driver.

The other piece worth watching closely is Ethiopia. Safaricom's Ethiopian operation has been a genuine drag on group profitability for several years as the company built out network infrastructure in a large, underpenetrated market — but the losses there have been narrowing, with management guiding toward a path to breakeven. If Ethiopia actually gets there on the timeline management has indicated, it removes a real drag on group earnings and becomes, in time, a second growth engine roughly the size of a small country's entire telecom market. If it doesn't, or takes materially longer, that's a real source of downside risk to the growth story that the market is currently giving some credit for.

The bull case for Safaricom isn't really about Kenya anymore — Kenya is the proven, cash-generative core. The genuinely interesting question is whether Ethiopia becomes a second M-Pesa-scale success story or a prolonged drag on group returns.

The valuation question

Safaricom trades at a trailing P/E in the neighbourhood of 14–15x, which sits below its own roughly decade-long historical median in the high teens. In isolation, that suggests the market isn't currently paying an unusually rich premium for the stock relative to its own history — if anything, current levels have sat somewhat below the historical average multiple in recent periods.

Whether that multiple is "cheap" or "fair" depends heavily on what you think happens with Ethiopia and with continued margin expansion in the core Kenyan business. Analyst forward-looking estimates have generally pointed toward continued earnings growth over the next couple of years, which — if realised — would mean the stock is effectively cheaper on a forward basis than the trailing multiple suggests, since the "E" in P/E would be growing faster than the price.

Set against a broader NSE market that has itself been trading at a discount to its own long-run historical average, Safaricom's premium relative to names like the major banks looks less extreme than it might first appear — the whole market has been re-rating from a low base, not just Safaricom in isolation.

The dividend side of the story

Safaricom's dividend track record is arguably as important to the investment case as the growth numbers. The company has paid a dividend every year since its 2008 listing without a miss — a genuinely rare record on the NSE, and one that matters a great deal to the large base of retail Kenyan shareholders who bought in specifically for reliable income. The trailing yield sits in the mid-single digits, which is modest compared to some of the higher-yielding banks and consumer names on the exchange, but it comes with a payout ratio that has generally left the company enough retained earnings to keep funding network investment and the Ethiopian build-out simultaneously — which is precisely the balance a maturing growth company should be striking.

The risks that actually matter

Regulatory risk sits at the top of the list. M-Pesa is systemically important to the Kenyan economy at this point, and that scale invites regulatory attention — on transaction fees, on competition policy, and on financial services regulation as M-Pesa increasingly functions like a bank in a lot of practical respects without being regulated as one. Any material regulatory intervention on pricing or market structure would hit the core of the investment case directly.

Ethiopia execution risk is the second major factor, as covered above — losses have been narrowing, but currency instability, competitive dynamics with the state incumbent, and the general difficulty of scaling telecom infrastructure in a large, complex market mean this is not a guaranteed outcome on any fixed timeline.

Competitive pressure within Kenya itself is a slower-moving but real risk — from other mobile money providers, from banks building their own digital payment rails, and from any future entrant with sufficient capital to attempt a genuine assault on M-Pesa's position. To date, none of these have meaningfully dented Safaricom's market position, but "to date" is doing some work in that sentence, and it's worth continuing to monitor rather than assuming permanently.

Where this leaves an investor

Safaricom in 2026 looks like a business with a genuine, demonstrable moat, improving core profitability, and a credible — if not yet fully proven — second growth leg in Ethiopia, trading at a multiple that sits at or below its own historical average. That's a reasonably attractive combination on the numbers. It isn't a stock without risk — regulatory exposure and Ethiopian execution are both real, ongoing questions rather than settled ones — but the core Kenyan business has earned the benefit of the doubt through nearly two decades of consistent execution and an unbroken dividend record. Whether that's enough to justify a position depends on your own read of the Ethiopian opportunity and your tolerance for regulatory uncertainty around Africa's most important fintech platform.
