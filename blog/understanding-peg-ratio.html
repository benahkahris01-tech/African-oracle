The PEG ratio:
the number that separates cheap from undervalued

Two stocks can share the exact same P/E and be worlds apart in value. PEG is the quick fix for that blind spot — and it matters more on a growth-heavy market like the NSE than almost anywhere else.

← Back to all articles
May 2026 · The African Oracle Research Team · Education

We've already gone through why a P/E ratio alone can mislead you. The PEG ratio is the simplest fix for its biggest blind spot: it doesn't know anything about growth. A stock growing earnings 3% a year and a stock growing earnings 25% a year can trade on the exact same P/E, and the P/E will tell you absolutely nothing about which one is the better deal. That's precisely the gap PEG was built to close.

The calculation, and why it works

PEG stands for Price/Earnings to Growth. You calculate it by dividing the P/E ratio by the company's expected earnings growth rate, expressed as a whole number rather than a percentage. A stock on a P/E of 15 growing earnings at 15% a year has a PEG of 1.0. A stock on a P/E of 25 growing at 25% has the exact same PEG of 1.0, even though its P/E looks far more expensive on its own.

The logic underneath this is straightforward once you see it: a higher P/E is justified — arguably fully justified — if the company is growing fast enough to earn its way into that multiple. Peter Lynch, the fund manager usually credited with popularising the ratio, used a rough rule of thumb that a PEG around 1.0 represents fair value, below 1.0 suggests a stock may be undervalued relative to its growth, and above 1.0 suggests the market may be paying a premium for that growth that isn't fully justified by the numbers.

A PEG below 1 doesn't automatically mean "buy." It means the market is pricing this company's growth more cheaply than a plain rule of thumb would suggest — which is worth investigating, not blindly trusting.

Why this matters more on the NSE than in developed markets

NSE-listed companies span an unusually wide range of growth rates for a single exchange — from mature, low-single-digit-growth utilities to businesses compounding earnings at 20%+ a year on the back of a growing, increasingly formalised economy. That spread makes P/E comparisons across the market almost useless without adjusting for growth, because you're routinely comparing a slow-grower on a low multiple against a fast-grower on a higher one, and P/E alone can't tell you which is actually the better value.

Take the banking sector we've discussed elsewhere on this site. NCBA and KCB have both delivered strong multi-year EPS growth in recent periods while trading at relatively modest P/E multiples in the mid-single digits. If that growth is sustained anywhere close to recent rates, the PEG on those names comes out meaningfully below 1.0 — the kind of setup that value-with-growth investors specifically look for. A slower-growing bank on the same headline P/E would show a considerably higher, less attractive PEG.

Where PEG genuinely helps

  • Comparing across growth profiles within a sector. Screening banks, telecoms or consumer names against each other on PEG rather than raw P/E surfaces names the market may be under-pricing relative to their own growth trajectory.
  • Sanity-checking a "growth story." If a stock is being pitched to you as a growth opportunity, PEG is a quick way to check whether the price already reflects most of that growth, or whether there's genuine room left for the market to re-rate the shares as growth continues to show up in the numbers.
  • Catching quietly cheap compounders. Sometimes a company keeps growing earnings steadily for years while its share price lags, quietly pushing the PEG down without anyone paying much attention. These are exactly the situations disciplined screening is designed to surface.

Where PEG breaks down

The ratio has real limitations, and treating it as gospel is its own kind of mistake.

Growth forecasts are opinions, not facts. The "G" in PEG usually comes from analyst estimates or a trailing growth rate extrapolated forward, and both are just educated guesses about the future. A single bad forecast can make an expensive stock look artificially cheap on a PEG basis, or a genuinely well-priced stock look expensive if analysts are being overly conservative.

It ignores risk entirely. Two companies can show the identical PEG while carrying completely different levels of business risk — different debt loads, different earnings quality, different exposure to a single customer or commodity. PEG says nothing about any of that. A fast-growing but heavily indebted company and a fast-growing, debt-free company can post the same PEG and deserve very different valuations.

It doesn't work for negative or near-zero growth. If a company's growth rate is negative or close to zero, the PEG calculation either turns negative or becomes meaninglessly large, and the ratio stops being useful. You need a different framework entirely for turnaround stories or structurally declining businesses.

Short growth bursts distort it. A company coming off a low base — recovering from a bad year, or benefiting from a one-off contract — can show an unsustainably high growth rate that flatters the PEG for a year or two before reverting to something much more ordinary. Always sanity-check whether the growth rate feeding the calculation is durable or a temporary blip.

How we'd actually use it

PEG is best treated as a second-stage filter, not a first-stage decision tool. Start with a normal P/E screen to narrow down candidates, layer PEG on top to see which of those candidates are more reasonably priced relative to their own growth, and then do the actual qualitative work — reading the last few years of results, checking the debt position via the metrics we cover in our piece on financial strength, and forming your own view on whether the growth rate is likely to hold up. A low PEG earns your attention. It doesn't earn your money on its own.
