NSE banking sector:
who actually makes the most money?

Equity, KCB, Co-op, NCBA, Absa — all profitable, all growing. But they are not built the same. We ran through the financials of Kenya's five largest listed banks so you do not have to.

← Back to all articles
May 2026 · The African Oracle Research Team · NSE Kenya

The NSE banking sector is the most liquid and most analysed part of the Kenyan stock market. Banks dominate the exchange's top 10 by market capitalisation. They consistently rank among the most traded counters on any given day. And yet a surprising number of investors who own banking stocks have not sat down and worked through what makes one better than another.

This article does that. We look at the five largest listed Kenyan banks — Equity Group, KCB Group, Co-operative Bank, NCBA Group, and Absa Bank Kenya — across five dimensions: earnings growth over five years, net interest margin and profitability, dividend yield, valuation (P/E), and the regional expansion story. By the end you should have a clearer sense of what you are actually buying when you pick one over the others.

A disclosure before we start: nothing here is a recommendation to buy or sell any share. These are analytical observations based on publicly available data. Make your own judgement.

Five-year EPS growth: the single most important indicator

If you had to pick one number to summarise a bank's recent performance, earnings per share growth over five years is probably it. It captures revenue growth, cost management, credit quality, and capital efficiency all in a single trajectory.

NSE Banks — 5-Year EPS Growth (FY2020 to FY2024/25)
Equity Group (EQTY) .... KES 5.24 → KES 12.33 ...... +135%
KCB Group (KCB) ........ KES 4.47 → KES 16.67 ...... +273%
Co-op Bank (COOP) ...... KES 1.71 → KES 5.04 ....... +195%
NCBA Group (NCBA) ...... KES 3.63 → KES 14.05 ...... +287%
Absa Bank Kenya (ABSA) . KES 1.22 → KES 4.22 ....... +246%

Source: Company annual reports. FY2025 data where available, FY2024 otherwise.

The standout here is NCBA, which delivered 287% EPS growth over five years. The merger of NIC Group and Commercial Bank of Africa in 2019 created significant integration cost savings that flowed directly to the bottom line as the combined entity matured. KCB's 273% growth is also remarkable given its size — this is not a small bank growing off a low base. That is a massive institution compounding earnings at a rate that rivals much smaller, more nimble competitors.

Equity Group's 135% looks modest by comparison but needs context: Equity was already a well-run bank in 2020 with a higher starting EPS base than most peers. Growing from a position of strength is harder than growing from a position of weakness.

Profitability: net margins and what they reveal

Net profit margin — the percentage of total income that ends up as profit after all costs — tells you about operational efficiency and pricing power. For banks specifically, it reflects how well they manage the spread between what they pay depositors and what they charge borrowers, after accounting for bad debts and operating costs.

NSE Banks — Net Profit Margin (FY2020 vs FY2024/25)
Equity Group (EQTY) .... 32% → 29% .... slight decline
KCB Group (KCB) ........ 25% → 34% .... strong improvement
Co-op Bank (COOP) ...... 28% → 35% .... strong improvement
NCBA Group (NCBA) ...... 24% → 37% .... exceptional improvement
Absa Bank Kenya (ABSA) . 22% → 34% .... strong improvement

Source: Company annual reports. Check current values on The African Oracle screener.

Four of the five banks improved their net margins meaningfully over the five-year period. NCBA's jump from 24% to 37% is the most dramatic — again, the merger efficiency story. But Co-op Bank's move from 28% to 35% is arguably more impressive because it came without the catalyst of a major merger. That is genuine operational improvement driven by digital banking adoption and disciplined cost management.

Equity's slight margin decline from 32% to 29% is worth watching. A bank with Equity's brand, scale, and management quality should not be losing margin. The group's aggressive continental expansion — particularly the acquisition of BCDC in the DRC — has added complexity and cost. Whether those investments pay off will determine whether Equity's margin profile improves again over the next five years.

Dividends: income investors take note

Most NSE bank investors are in it partly for the dividend. Banks are typically the highest-yielding sector on the exchange given their profitability and relatively lower growth reinvestment requirements compared to, say, a telecommunications company building out network infrastructure.

NCBA Group (NCBA) .......... KES 5.50 DPS ... ~5.1% yield
Equity Group (EQTY) ........ KES 4.25 DPS ... ~6.2% yield
Absa Bank Kenya (ABSA) ..... KES 1.75 DPS ... ~4.8% yield
Co-op Bank (COOP) .......... KES 1.50 DPS ... ~4.9% yield
KCB Group (KCB) ............ KES 3.00 DPS ... ~4.4% yield

Yields are indicative based on current prices. Check live yields at africanoracle.info

Standard Chartered Kenya stands out on yield — and on absolute DPS — but it is worth being clear about why. SCBK has a relatively small float and a high share price. The KES 22.50 DPS reflects its premium positioning and higher per-share earnings base rather than unusually generous payout policies relative to earnings. Its payout ratio is not dramatically different from peers.

For income investors, Equity Group combines a reasonable yield of around 6% with a far superior growth trajectory compared to SCBK. That combination — income plus growth — is usually the more attractive long-term proposition, though SCBK investors would reasonably argue that predictability has its own premium.

Valuation: who is cheapest and does it matter?

Every NSE bank looks cheap on a P/E basis relative to global banking peers. We have already explained in our P/E article why that comparison has limits. But within the NSE banking sector, relative P/E differences do tell you something about how the market is pricing the different risk and return profiles of each institution.

KCB and Co-op Bank typically trade at the lowest multiples — around 4 to 5x. Both are carrying regional expansion risk and, in KCB's case, integration risk from multiple acquisitions. NCBA trades at a slightly higher multiple given its earnings growth momentum. Equity trades at a premium to the group given its brand, management quality, and diversified business model. SCBK trades at the highest multiple, reflecting its stable, high-margin, internationally-backed business.

None of these is obviously wrong. They are rational assessments of different risk-return combinations. The question for investors is which combination fits their own investment profile.

Regional expansion: blessing or distraction?

The regional expansion narrative is probably the most important long-term differentiator between these banks, and also the hardest to analyse from publicly available information alone.

Equity Group is the most aggressive regional player, with operations in seven countries and the DRC acquisition representing a major long-term bet on Central Africa's growth potential. KCB is also aggressively regional, having acquired National Bank of Kenya, BancABC Tanzania, and Banque Populaire du Rwanda in quick succession. NCBA is expanding more selectively. Co-op Bank remains primarily Kenya-focused, which is both a source of stability and a growth ceiling.

Regional expansion in African banking is not inherently good or bad. It depends entirely on execution. And execution in markets like the DRC, South Sudan, and Ethiopia is genuinely difficult.

The DRC is a particularly interesting case study for Equity. The country has one of Africa's lowest rates of formal banking penetration — under 20% of adults have bank accounts — which means the opportunity is enormous. But operating costs are high, currency risk is real (the Congolese franc has historically been volatile), and regulatory complexity is significant. Equity's management has the track record to deserve the benefit of the doubt. But the DRC contribution to group earnings needs to be monitored carefully by investors.

The bottom line: each bank for a different investor

There is no single "best" NSE bank for every investor. But here is roughly how we would characterise each for a specific investment objective:

For earnings growth: NCBA and KCB have delivered the strongest five-year EPS growth trajectories. NCBA's merger-driven efficiency gains may moderate from here, but KCB's regional scale is still building momentum.

For income: Equity Group and Standard Chartered Kenya offer the best combination of yield and business quality. Co-op Bank is a solid income option for investors who prioritise predictability.

For value: KCB and Co-op Bank trade at the lowest multiples and have both demonstrated improving profitability. At current prices, KCB in particular looks interesting for investors willing to accept regional execution risk.

For quality and stability: Equity Group remains the benchmark NSE bank for business quality — wide moat, strong management, diversified revenue, and a 20+ year track record of earnings growth. It will rarely be the cheapest, but it is rarely a mistake to own it.
